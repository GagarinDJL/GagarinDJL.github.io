abstract: >-
  Influence Maximization (IM) in online social networks has traditionally been formulated as selecting a budgeted set of seed users to maximize expected spread under a diffusion model. This classical view enabled foundational hardness, submodularity, and approximation results, but it is increasingly insufficient for realistic online social systems, where users differ in behaviors and opinions, networks evolve, diffusion is context-dependent, objectives go beyond coverage, and influence may generate negative, unfair, privacy-sensitive, or harmful consequences. This survey provides a unified review of IM from the perspective of trustworthy influence decision-making. We first develop a six-layer Online Social Influence-Maximization Stack that links network representation, behavior and context, diffusion mechanisms, objective design, algorithmic optimization, and governance. We then synthesize diffusion and objective models through a model–objective–algorithm perspective, covering classical, context-aware, behavioral, economic, opinion-aware, competitive, harm-aware, and learned diffusion settings. Next, we compare algorithmic paradigms ranging from greedy and lazy-greedy methods to sampling, sketching, mathematical programming, evolutionary search, non-submodular search, learning-based seed selection, and adaptive policies, with respect to their theoretical guarantees, time and space complexity, and information requirements. We further discuss realistic online social network settings, including dynamics, heterogeneity, multiplexity, cyber–physical contexts, partial observability, and group-level influence. Finally, we identify robustness, fairness, privacy, harm control, causal validity, explainability, accountability, benchmarking, datasets, and reproducibility as central requirements for next-generation IM, with particular attention to their interaction with scalability. Across these developments, four tensions recur: realism versus tractability, prediction versus optimization, observability and privacy versus scalable estimation, and trustworthiness versus scalability. This survey argues that IM should be understood as trustworthy, adaptive, and utility-aware influence decision-making in complex social systems, rather than as spread-only seed selection.
